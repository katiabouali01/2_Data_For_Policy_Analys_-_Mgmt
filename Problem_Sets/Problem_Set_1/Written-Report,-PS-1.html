describe_num <- function(x) {
  x <- x[!is.na(x)]
  data.frame(
    n = length(x), mean = mean(x), sd = sd(x), median = median(x),
    min = min(x), max = max(x), range = max(x) - min(x),
    skew = mean((x - mean(x))^3) / sd(x)^3
  )
}

freq_table <- function(x) {
  x <- x[!is.na(x)]
  tab <- table(x)
  data.frame(category = names(tab), n = as.integer(tab),
             pct = round(100 * as.integer(tab) / length(x), 1))
}

Question 2: Significance Tests- Hourly Wage and Workforce Characteristics Because wage is strongly right-skewed (skewed ~ 5 –> mean $15.33 vs. median $12.83), it violates the normality assumption behind t-tests, ANOVA, and Pearson correlation. Nonparametric tests, which compare ranks or distributions rather than means, were used consistently across all give analyses below.

Wage and Staff Role A Kruskal-Wallis test found a statistically significant difference in wage across staff roles, x^(2)=107.75, p<.001. Median hourly wage was lowest for aides/assistant teachers ($12.00, m=1,469), higher for teachers/lead teachers ($13.40, n=2,785), and highest for the small “other/undetermined” group ($15.50, n=16). This indicates that staff role is significantly associated with pay, with support level positions earning less than lead teaching positions.

kruskal.test(wage ~ role, data = nsece)

Methods Choice of statistical methods–> Two families of significance tests are commonly used ofr the relationships examined in Q2: parametric tests (Pearson correlation, independent samples t-test, one-way ANOVA) and their nonparametric counterparts Spearman rank correlation, Wilcoxon Mann Whitney test, Kruskal Wallis test). The parametric tests assume the outcome variable is approximately normally distributed within each group or across the relationship being tested. violations of this assumption can distort p-values and standard errors, particularly when a distribution is heavily skewed. As established in Q1, hourly wage is strongly right skewed (skewness ~ 5, mean $15.33 vs median $12.83) which violates the normality assumption underlying the parametric tests.

For this reason, nonparametric alternatives were used throughout Q2, consistent with the guidance in Agresti (2018): nonparametric methods “are also useful when the two groups have highly skewed distributions, because then the mean may not be a meaningful summary measure” (Ch 7.7) Specifically:

The Kriskal Wallis test was used in place of one-way ANOVA for comparing wage across staff role (3 groups) and race (4 groups). Rather than comparing group means (as ANOVA does), Kruskal Wallis ranks all observations and compares mean ranks across groups, making it robust to the skew and outliers present in the wage variable (Agresti, 2018 Ch.12.3)

The Spearman rank correlation was used in place of Pearson correlation for the relationsips between wage and the two ordinal variables (years worked, educational attainment). Pearson correlation assumes a linear relationship between two continous, normally distributed variables. Spearman instead measures the strength of a monotonic relationship using ranks, which is appropriate both because wage is skewed and because years worked and education are ordinal rather than truly continuous variables

The Wilcoxon rank sum (Mann Whitney) test was used in place of an independent-samples t-test for comparing wage between genders. Like Kriskal Wallis, it compares rank distributions rather than means, and is the standard nonparametric substitue for a two-group test (Agresti, 2018, Ch 7.7)

Using nonparametric tests consistently across all five analyses also has a secondary benefit–> it keeps the choice of test uniform across Q2 rather than switching justification variable by variable and it also aligns with reporting medians, rather than means, as the primary descriptive statistic for wage establishment in Q1.

Question 3: Policy Implications Taken togeher, the analyses point to several segments of the ECE workforce most affected by low wages. First, staff role matters: aides and assitant teachers earn a significantly lower median wage than teachers and lead teachers (p<.001), suggesting compensation policy should not treat “ECE educator” as a single undifferentiated category –> entry-level and support staff face a distinct wage disadvantage. Second, race is strongly associated with wage: Black educators earn a notably lower median wage than their white, asian, and other race counterparts (p<.001), indicating that racial age disparities are a persistent and significant feature of this workforce, not incidental variation. Third, education attainment shows the strongest positive association with wage of the ordinal predictors tested (p=.48, p<.001), meaning workers with lower levels of formal education are systematically concentrated among the lowest earners, even though the ECE workforce already has substantial postsecondary attainment relative to its pay. By contrast, years of experience shows only a weak positive relationship with wage (p=.28), suggesting that tenure alone does little to lift wages, a structural problem, since it implies the field offers limited return on staying in the profession long term. Evidence for a gender wage gap was comparatively weak: while the Man-Whitney test reached conventional significance (p=.043), the male subgroup was very small (n=96 vs 4,133 women), so this result should be interpreted catiously rather than treated as a robust finding.

Given these findings and patterns, policy interventions aimed at improving ECE compensation should prioritize: 1. raising wage floors specifically for aide and assistant-teacher roles rather than only lead teachers, sine role-based stratification is one of the clearest drivers of low pay. 2. directly addressing the racial wage gap affecting Black educators, since this disparity persists independent of role or education, and 3. building compensation structures that reward educational credentials more consistently and reward tenure more meaningfully, since the current wage structure does relatively little to compensate experience. Given the very small male sub sample, any policy response to a possible gender gap would benefit from a larger, more balanced sample before being prioritized. It is worth noting that the ‘other’ race category combines several distinct groups (including multiracial respondents) so this racial wage gap analysis understates the full complexity of racial disparities in the ECE workforce and should be treated as a starting point rather than a complete picture
